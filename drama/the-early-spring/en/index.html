Act One · Beijing
EP 1—8

On her first day, he told her to quit

Shang Zhitao’s résumé was ordinary, yet she landed an offer at a top advertising agency. On her first day, creative director Luan Nian took one look at her and told her, plainly, not to bother.

That moment fixed their heights. One of them hands down the verdict; the other waits for it.

Most workplace romances use rank as a prop for tension. This one uses it as the foundation.

How the scale tipped over ten years

On one side, a peach — táo, the last character of Shang Zhitao’s name. On the other, an L, for Luan Nian. On a scale the heavier side sinks: first the L, then the peach, and at last the two weigh the same. Drag the slider, or tap a stage.

One sentence of his outweighs all of her

On day one, a creative director’s “don’t bother” is enough to decide whether she stays. His side sinks all the way down.

Act Four · Harbin
EP 17—24

By the time he came looking, she no longer needed him

Years later Luan Nian travels north. By then Shang Zhitao has long left the grind behind, has opened her own shop back home, and her days belong to her.

The order is what matters. She wasn’t waiting where he left her — she had finished rebuilding her life before he showed up. The grovelling-ex arc only works on that condition.

Former boss, former lover, face to face again — and the whole structure of power has turned over.

THREE LINES ・ From the novel

Three lines, and exactly three turning points

The following are quoted from the novel, not spoken on screen. Open each for a reading and what to watch for.

Ⅰ May your life be free of worry, and may you always have the courage to break a deadlock A blessing ・ Opening

The weight is in the second half. “A life free of worry” is the polite thing anyone says. “Courage to break a deadlock” isn’t a wish for smooth sailing — it assumes the deadlock is coming.

Deadlock is daily life in advertising: a pitch stalls, an idea is killed, the client won’t nod. Put it back on the romance and those six years are a deadlock too, one neither of them will break first. What the line wishes for isn’t that nothing happens — it’s that when something does, you can still move.

Watch for: if this becomes a generic well-wish on screen, the character’s whole undertone changes.

Ⅱ This isn’t fleeing. This is a grand retreat. Goodbye, Beijing Leaving without a word ・ Development

“Retreat” is military language, not a loser’s word — planned, commanded, forces preserved. Fleeing is the rout. She gives her leaving a proper name first, and only then says goodbye.

The crucial word is “grand.” A departure with no one to see her off, one he doesn’t even know about — and she insists on giving it a scale. Not for anyone else to hear, but so she remembers: I chose this. I didn’t lose.

Watch for: a novel can write interior monologue; screen can’t. This feeling has to be carried by image or voiceover.

Ⅲ Let’s meet each other again — starting from the first time we met Reunion ・ Resolution

He isn’t asking to get back together. He’s asking to start over. The difference matters: getting back together returns to the old relationship; starting over admits the old starting point was the problem.

Their first meeting was him, as creative director, telling her to give up the job. The heights were set in that instant. So “from the first time we met” isn’t a romantic flourish — it’s him finally realising that the only way to reach eye level is to void the starting point where she had to look up. The whole argument of the work lands in this line.

Watch for: keep this line, put it in the right place, and the adaptation is more than half won.

CODA

Early spring isn’t a season. It’s a temperature

The snow is loosening, but there is still ice underfoot. The phrase holds Beijing’s winter, Harbin’s snow, and a relationship that stayed for years on the edge of thawing without ever fully melting. The title is the largest image in the whole work.
